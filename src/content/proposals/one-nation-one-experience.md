## In brief

India’s federal structure allows different governments and institutions to respond to different regional needs. That diversity does not require every citizen-facing process to feel fragmented.

Someone moving between states should not have to repeatedly establish the same identity, rebuild the administrative history of an asset, or learn an entirely new process for an equivalent public service. Someone changing a regulated service provider should not have to resubmit information that has already been securely verified.

> Keep the federal structure. Keep state authority. Unify the citizen experience.

“One Nation, One Experience” is therefore not a proposal for one database, one department, or one rule for every circumstance. It is a proposal for common standards, portable credentials, interoperable systems, and a simple experience at the point where a citizen meets an institution.

## Context

Administrative complexity is often invisible to institutions because citizens perform the integration themselves. They carry documents between offices, submit the same information repeatedly, obtain new certificates, reconcile inconsistent records, and discover which authority controls each part of a journey.

Digitising each form independently does not necessarily solve that problem. A fragmented process can remain fragmented even when every step is online. The deeper question is whether authorised systems can verify facts, exchange the minimum necessary information, and coordinate responsibility without asking the citizen to start again.

India already has examples of portability and shared digital infrastructure. The lesson is not that every service should copy one technical platform. It is that the country can define common citizen outcomes while allowing different institutions to implement and govern their parts.

## The blueprint

Establish a national interoperability compact organised around five promises.

### 1. Tell an institution once

When a fact has already been verified by an authorised source, another service should request the citizen’s consent to retrieve or verify that fact instead of demanding another copy. The citizen should be able to see what is requested, why it is needed, and for how long it will be retained.

### 2. Carry credentials across boundaries

Licences, registrations, qualifications, and entitlements should remain verifiable when a citizen moves between states or changes the institution delivering a service. Portability does not mean that every credential grants identical rights everywhere; it means its authenticity and current status can travel without being recreated.

### 3. Switch without starting again

Changing an eligible service provider should be a consent-driven transfer, not a fresh identity exercise. The new provider can still perform required checks, disclose terms, and obtain explicit approval, but the interaction should reuse verified information wherever law and security permit.

### 4. Make correction portable too

Interoperability must include a clear way to challenge an incorrect record, identify the responsible authority, propagate an authorised correction, and obtain human review. Connecting systems without connecting remedies would make errors more powerful.

### 5. Design standards for the unknown

Public infrastructure should distinguish enduring capabilities from today’s categories. Identity, ownership, safety, liability, location, and access standards should be extensible enough to accommodate autonomous vehicles, connected infrastructure, delivery robots, drones, and new forms of mobility without rebuilding the system for every invention.

## Implementation

The programme should begin with citizen journeys, not a new national portal. Select recurring situations—moving home, changing a provider, transferring a vehicle, proving a professional qualification—and document every form, verification, payment, visit, delay, and hand-off involved.

For each journey, participating institutions would agree on:

1. the outcome a citizen should experience;
2. which authority remains responsible for each decision;
3. the minimum data needed at each step;
4. a common method for consent, verification, status updates, and appeals;
5. service standards for time, accessibility, and offline assistance; and
6. a process for adding new states, providers, and future categories without central redesign.

The architecture should generally be federated. Information can remain with the institution responsible for it while other authorised systems receive a signed verification or purpose-limited response. A shared experience does not require a universal profile containing everything about a person.

## Telecom as an instructive example

A mobile number can already remain useful across regions, and mobile-number portability allows a subscriber to retain that number when changing service providers. That is an important form of continuity: the citizen’s relationship with family, work, banking, and public services does not need to change merely because the network provider changes.

But the provider-switching experience can still improve. The present portability process requires the subscriber to obtain a Unique Porting Code, submit a customer-acquisition and porting form with KYC information to the new operator, receive another SIM, and wait for the transfer to complete.

The 2075 objective should be a secure, largely digital flow: authenticate, compare and accept the new terms, give explicit consent, and transfer the service with clear status updates. Existing verified information should be reused where legally permitted rather than collected as paper or uploaded again.

“At the click of a button” should describe the citizen’s experience, not the absence of safeguards. The systems behind that action must still prevent SIM-swap fraud, unauthorised transfers, identity theft, and evasion of legitimate obligations.

## Special cases to explore

### Vehicle registration

A vehicle should receive one national registration for its entire lifecycle. After purchase and initial registration, an interstate move should not require the owner to re-register it, obtain intergovernmental clearances, or personally reconcile state taxes and refunds. The owner reports a genuine change once; authorised state and central systems handle jurisdiction, regulation, enforcement, and revenue settlement behind the scenes.

Registration, ownership, finance interests, insurance, safety, emissions compliance, enforcement, and state revenue settlement make this a valuable test of a unified citizen experience operating across a federal system.

### Personal and professional credentials

Driving licences, educational qualifications, professional licences, and other credentials should be independently verifiable across jurisdictions. Recognition rules may differ, but authenticity should not depend on carrying paper back to the issuing authority.

### Benefits and essential services

Eligible benefits and essential records should not disappear when a person crosses a state boundary. Portability must be designed with local eligibility rules, fiscal responsibility, and protection against exclusion clearly stated.

## Benefits and intended beneficiaries

- Less repetitive paperwork, travel, and dependence on intermediaries.
- Faster access to services after moving between states.
- Easier competition and consumer choice where providers can be changed.
- Fewer inconsistent copies of the same information.
- Clearer responsibility when a record is wrong or a process fails.
- Infrastructure that can absorb new technologies and service categories.

The greatest benefit should reach people whose work, study, family, or livelihood requires mobility. An experience that works only for confident smartphone users would not meet the proposal’s standard.

## Trade-offs

**Federal autonomy.** Common experience standards must not quietly transfer policy decisions from states to the Union. Authorities should retain their lawful powers while committing to interoperability and published service outcomes.

**Privacy and surveillance.** Easier exchange can become excessive exchange. Purpose limitation, data minimisation, consent, access logs, retention limits, and independent oversight are fundamental requirements.

**Cybersecurity and concentration.** A single central platform could create an attractive target and a national point of failure. Federated verification and resilient offline procedures may be safer than central storage.

**Exclusion through bad data.** Interoperability can spread an error faster. Human review, correction, appeal, and continuity of essential service must be part of the infrastructure.

**Uneven institutional capacity.** Smaller departments and local bodies may need funding, shared standards, and technical assistance. A national experience cannot depend on every institution having identical resources.

## Open questions

- Which five citizen journeys currently impose the greatest cost through repeated verification or incompatible systems?
- What should be portable nationally, and what should remain intentionally local?
- Which facts can be verified without being copied into another database?
- How should consent work when a service is essential and refusal is not a realistic choice?
- Who is responsible when two authoritative systems disagree?
- What minimum offline experience must every participating service provide?
- How should common standards be governed so that states help design them rather than merely adopt them?
- Which safeguards would make near-instant telecom switching convenient without increasing fraud?

## What would change this proposal

Evidence that a proposed integration centralises power, increases exclusion, or creates disproportionate privacy and security risks should favour a narrower verification standard rather than full data exchange. Where an existing system already provides a genuinely portable experience, effort should focus on adoption, reliability, and remedies instead of replacing it.
