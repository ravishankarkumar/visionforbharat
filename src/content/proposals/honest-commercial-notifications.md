## In brief

A commercial notification may be written to resemble a message from a loved one, a salary alert, an urgent account event, or a system warning even when its actual purpose is to advertise a product or provoke an app opening.

Phrases such as “Your Valentine” or “Salary credited” can exploit expectations that normally deserve immediate attention. Creativity in advertising should not depend on disguising the identity or purpose of the speaker.

## Context

Notifications interrupt people outside the app that sends them. The lock-screen preview often appears before a person can see the full context, giving a few words unusual power to imitate a personal or consequential message.

The problem is not humour, informality, or persuasive writing by itself. The relevant question is whether a reasonable recipient could initially mistake a promotion for a personal communication, financial event, emergency, security warning, or device message.

## The blueprint

Create a clear rule against materially misleading commercial-notification previews. Promotional messages should identify the sender and commercial purpose in the text visible to the recipient.

The rule should focus on deceptive presentation rather than maintaining a brittle list of forbidden phrases. Regulators could publish examples and update guidance as designs change.

## Implementation

App stores and operating systems could provide a standard promotional-notification category, while the appropriate consumer-protection authority defines and enforces the underlying obligation. Users should be able to report a misleading notification directly from the notification interface with the relevant text and sender attached.

Repeated or deliberate violations could lead to escalating penalties, restrictions on promotional notifications, or app-store enforcement. An initial framework should distinguish marketing from genuine transactional alerts requested by the user.

## Trade-offs

An excessively broad rule could flatten legitimate brand voice or punish ambiguous jokes. Enforcement therefore needs a reasonable-person standard, published examples, an appeal process, and attention to the full notification context.

## Open questions

- Which authority should define and enforce the standard?
- What visible disclosure is sufficient on a small lock screen?
- How should mixed transactional and promotional messages be classified?
- Can reporting be made simple without enabling coordinated abuse?
